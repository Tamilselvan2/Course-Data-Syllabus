Core Definitions & Etymology

The word Entrepreneur derives from the French Entreprendre, meaning "to undertake." Entrepreneurship is the process of creating something new with value by devoting time and effort, assuming financial and social risks, and receiving rewards.

Joseph Schumpeter
Proposed "Creative Destruction" — innovative entrepreneurs continuously destroy old market structures to create new ones.
Peter Drucker
Defined an entrepreneur as one who shifts economic resources out of lower productivity into higher productivity and greater yield.
🎯 Exam Definition

"Entrepreneurship is the process of starting a new business by identifying opportunities, organizing resources, taking risks, and earning profit."

Classification of Entrepreneurs
Very Very Important

Clarence Danhof's Classification based on economic development:

1. Innovating Entrepreneurs

Aggressively assembles information and introduces novel combinations of production. Enters market with products based on new technology.

2. Adoptive / Imitative

Copies or adopts suitable innovations made by innovative entrepreneurs to suit their local contexts.

3. Fabian Entrepreneurs

Shy and lazy by nature. Neither introduces new changes nor adopts new methods easily. Remains passive until forced by loss of market share.

4. Drone Entrepreneurs

Refuses to copy or use opportunities. Strictly conventional — sticks to set practices even if facing losses.

Other Crucial Classifications:
By Business Type
  • Trading — Trading activities, not manufacturing
  • Industrial — Manufacturers who identify needs
  • Agricultural — Mechanization, irrigation, crop tech
  • Corporate — Within large organizations
By Technology & Activity
  • Technical — Heavy tech innovation
  • Non-technical — Alternative strategies
  • Novice — First business
  • Serial — Starts, grows, exits multiple
  • Portfolio — Owns multiple simultaneously
Special Types
  • First-Generation — Starts from scratch, no family background
  • Social Entrepreneur — Solves community/social problems
  • Women Entrepreneur — Women-led ventures
  • Technopreneur — Technology-based startups
Social Entrepreneurship

Business activity aimed at solving social problems: rural education, low-cost healthcare, eco-friendly products.

Benefits: Social welfare, employment, rural development.

Government Schemes & Institutional Support
India Focus

Key schemes and institutions for Indian entrepreneurs:

Startup India
Launched 2016. Tax exemptions, funding support, easier registration, self-certification on labor/environmental laws for 3 years.
Stand-Up India
Financial loan assistance targeting SC/ST communities and women entrepreneurs.
MUDRA Yojana
Micro Units Development & Refinance Agency. Accessible refinance support to micro-enterprises.
PMEGP
Prime Minister's Employment Generation Programme. Financial subsidy and credit for micro-manufacturing units.
SIDBI
Small Industries Development Bank of India. Primary body providing refined credit lines to small businesses.
NSIC & KVIC
NSIC provides marketing/technical support. KVIC promotes rural employment via khadi and village industries.
🎯 DPIIT Framework

Entities recognized as startups up to a designated timeframe from inception. Grants self-certification on labor/environmental laws for 3 years and tax exemptions for 3 consecutive years.

Business Model & Value Proposition
Very Important

A Business Model explicitly describes how an organization creates, delivers, and captures value. Developed by Alexander Osterwalder.

Value Proposition: A statement that clearly identifies the bundle of products or services that creates value and benefits for a customer. It explains why customers should buy your product.

Value Proposition Examples
  • Faster delivery
  • Lower price
  • Better quality
  • Eco-friendly product
  • Convenience
  • Customization
Operational Business Models
  • Subscription — Predictable periodic payments
  • Freemium — Basic free, premium paid
  • Retailer / Manufacturer
  • Fee-for-Service
  • Product-as-a-Service
  • Leasing
  • Razor-and-Blade
⚠️ Fatal Flaw

A business model faces immediate structural destruction if it operates with no clear value proposition or an undefined revenue model.

🎨
Business Model Canvas — 9 Building Blocks
Most Repeated

The BMC contains 9 structural building blocks. Human Resource Policy is NOT one of them.

1. Customer Segments
Who are the customers? Mass, niche, local, global.
2. Value Proposition
What value is offered? Why should they buy?
3. Channels
How products reach customers. Website, retail, apps.
4. Customer Relationships
How customers are maintained. Support, loyalty.
5. Revenue Streams
How money is earned. Sales, subscription, ads.
6. Key Activities
Main business operations. Manufacturing, marketing.
7. Key Resources
Assets required. Physical, intellectual, human, financial.
8. Key Partners
Suppliers, partners, alliances. VARs, MSPs, OEMs.
9. Cost Structure
Business expenses. Fixed costs, variable costs, economies of scale.
📝 Exam Diagram

Draw a 3×3 grid. Place Value Proposition in the center. Left side: Partners, Activities, Resources. Right side: Segments, Relationships, Channels. Bottom: Cost Structure (left) and Revenue Streams (right).

Feasibility Analysis
Very Important

Checks whether a business idea is practical and profitable. A multi-layered study is mandatory before writing a business plan.

1. Technical Feasibility

Analyzes accessibility of technical resources, plant layout capacities, and process designs. Can we build it?

2. Financial / Economic Feasibility

Cost-benefit analysis. Reviews material costs, labor overheads, and potential revenue changes. Is it profitable?

3. Market / Industry Feasibility

Reviews market size, industry attractiveness, demand, competition, and barriers to entry. Will customers buy?

4. Organizational / Management Feasibility

Assesses whether the team possesses the management prowess and structural competence to execute the project.

5. Scheduling Feasibility

Can the project be completed on time? Examines timelines, milestones, and resource availability against deadlines.

🎯 Exam Recall

Technical + Financial + Market + Organizational + Scheduling

The Business Plan
Very Very Important

A formal document outlining goals, operational strategies, and financial projections. The Executive Summary must be written last but placed first.

Components of Business Plan
  1. Executive Summary
    Short overview (write last, place first)
  2. Company Description
    Mission, vision, history
  3. Market Analysis
    Industry outlook, target market
  4. Product/Service Description
    What you sell, lifecycle, R&D
  5. Marketing Plan
    Strategy, pricing, promotion
Remaining Components
  1. Operational Plan
    Location, facilities, supply chain
  2. Financial Plan
    Projections, BEP, cash flow
  3. Funding Requirement
    Capital needed, use of funds
  4. Appendix
    Resumes, permits, contracts
SWOT Analysis
  • Strengths — Internal advantages
  • Weaknesses — Internal limitations
  • Opportunities — External favorable factors
  • Threats — External risks
Lean Startup Methodology

Eliminates heavy upfront planning by deploying an MVP (Minimum Viable Product) — a basic version with just enough features to execute a validated Build-Measure-Learn feedback loop.

📊 Break-Even Point (BEP)

Calculated within financial projections to determine the precise volume where total revenue exactly equals total cost.

Identifies the longest sequence of dependent tasks that determines the absolute minimum project duration. Any delay here immediately delays final delivery.

Purpose: Reduces delay, helps scheduling, identifies critical tasks.

Financial Analysis & Metrics
Repeated

Evaluates profit, sales, expenses, and cash flow to ensure startup survival.

Cash Flow Projection

The most vital early-stage statement. Maps future cash inflows and outflows to track operational survival.

Balance Sheet

Financial snapshot at a specific date. If asset-to-liability ratio falls below 1:1, the startup faces high risk of immediate bankruptcy.

Burn Rate

The rate at which a pre-revenue startup spends its cash reserves to cover monthly overheads. Critical for runway calculation.

Bootstrapping

Starting and growing a business using purely personal savings and early customer revenues without external venture funding.

Budgeting

Planning and controlling financial resources to achieve goals.

Importance: Cost control • Profit planning • Resource allocation

Legal Aspects of Startups
Very Important
Business Structures
  • Sole Proprietorship — Single owner, unlimited liability, simple to establish
  • Partnership — Two or more owners, shared profits/liabilities
  • Private Limited Company — Separate legal entity, min 2 directors, governed by Companies Act 2013
  • LLP — Limited Liability Partnership. Combines partnership flexibility with corporate liability protection.
Mandatory Licenses
  • GST Registration — Unified indirect tax
  • Udyam / MSME — Credit protection & subsidies
  • Shop & Establishment Act — Commercial office license
  • FSSAI License — Mandatory for food businesses
  • Trade License
Intellectual Property Rights (IPR)
  • Patents — Functional innovations
  • Trademarks — Brand identity
  • Copyrights — Creative works
📋 IBC 2016

Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code enables faster, legally sound exit paths for failed startups.

Step-by-Step

Steps to launch a food delivery startup:

  2. Feasibility Analysis
